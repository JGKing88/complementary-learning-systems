# Place-cell firing in large environments: field size, field number and how both scale with environment size

Verification note for the report writer. I read the full text and supplements of Rich et al. 2014, Eliav et al. 2021 and Harland et al. 2021 (accepted manuscript) directly. For Fenton et al. 2008, Park et al. 2011, Tanni et al. 2022, Davidson et al. 2009 and Diba & Buzsáki 2008, an automated reader extracted the numbers from the full text, so a spot check against the PDFs is advisable. For Muller & Kubie 1987, O'Keefe & Burgess 1996, Kjelstrup et al. 2008, Jung et al. 1994, Lee et al. 2020 and Mainali et al. 2025 I only had the abstract or secondary text, and each such item is flagged.

## Q1. What the key studies report about field size, fields per cell, the distribution of field counts, field spacing, and change with environment size

### Takeaway
Every study that enlarged the environment found more fields per cell and more active cells. Field number per cell is overdispersed across cells (gamma-Poisson, a few cells with many fields and many with few or none), field locations along a cell's map look random (uniform positions, exponential gaps, no grid-like periodicity), and absolute field size grows with environment size but sublinearly, so each field covers a smaller fraction of a larger space. Bats in a 200 m tunnel added a property not reported in rodents: fields of one dorsal CA1 cell ranged up to 20-fold in size (0.6 to 32 m overall).

#### Fenton et al. 2008 (rat dorsal CA1, tetrodes)
- Environments were a 68 cm diameter cylinder (about 0.36 m²) and a "monkey chamber" with a 150 × 140 cm footprint (2.1 m²) and stairs on three sides. The abstract calls the chamber about six times larger; my arithmetic gives 21,000 / 3,632 cm² = 5.8×. Sessions lasted 15 min (cylinder) and 60 min (chamber). [Fenton 2008, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2695947/)
- N = 4 rats. 477 active pyramidal cells in the cylinder and 448 in the chamber; 307 and 322 of these were place cells (64% vs 72%, p < 0.001). Place-cell criteria were coherence > 0.25, information > 0.5 bits/AP and at least one field. [Fenton 2008, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2695947/)
- A field was at least 9 contiguous 4.7 × 4.7 cm pixels (about 22 cm² each) with rate > 0 AP/s, each pixel sharing a side with another field pixel. No smoothing is described. [Fenton 2008, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2695947/)
- Fields per cell were 1.3 ± 0.03 in the cylinder, 3.4 ± 0.11 on the chamber floor, and 4.05 when the stairs were included. 72% of cylinder place cells had one field versus 11% in the chamber (89% multi-field). [Fenton 2008, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2695947/)
- Field size was 57.8 ± 2.4 pixels in the cylinder versus 66.2 ± 3.5 (whole chamber) and 75.0 ± 3.9 pixels (chamber floor); for the same cells, 52.4 ± 3.5 versus 94.6 ± 8.9 pixels. The text says fields were "on average by 30%" larger in the chamber. In cm², 57.8 px × 22.09 cm² = about 1,280 cm² and 75.0 px = about 1,660 cm² (my arithmetic). [Fenton 2008, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2695947/)
- As a proportion of the environment, fields covered 0.27 ± 0.01 of the cylinder versus 0.055 ± 0.003 of the chamber and 0.073 ± 0.004 of the chamber floor. Arithmetic check: 1,660 / 21,000 cm² = 0.079, close to the reported 0.073, but 1,280 / 3,632 cm² = 0.35, not 0.27, so the cylinder proportion must use a different denominator or averaging that I could not confirm. [Fenton 2008, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2695947/)
- Multiple fields were irregular. Mean nearest-neighbour distance between a cell's fields was 11.5 ± 0.43 pixels (about 54 cm). The nearest autocorrelogram peak was at 21.4 ± 0.5 pixels in the chamber versus 10.1 ± 0.2 pixels in the cylinder, and only 7.6% of chamber cells had central-peak angles in the 50 to 70° range expected of a hexagonal grid. [Fenton 2008, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2695947/)
- Cells fired in less than 35% of the space. The proportion of active pixels was 0.41 (cylinder) versus 0.29 (chamber). Isolation quality explained only about 6% of the variance in field number. [Fenton 2008, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2695947/)

#### Park, Dvorak & Fenton 2011 (rat dorsal CA1, CA3 and DG, tetrodes)
- Environments were a 76 cm cylinder (0.45 m²) and a 1.8 × 1.4 m box (2.52 m², 5.6× the cylinder). Sessions were 15 min (cylinder) and 60 min (box). Three rats gave the electrophysiology. [Park 2011, PLoS ONE](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0022349)
- A field was at least 9 contiguous 2.8 cm pixels with rate > 0 AP/s, giving a minimum field area of about 70.6 cm² (9 × 7.84 cm²). [Park 2011, PLoS ONE](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0022349)
- Fields per cell in the box were 5.89 ± 0.40 (CA1, n = 104 cells from 3 rats), 4.96 ± 0.56 (CA3, n = 52, one rat) and 5.35 ± 0.38 (DG, n = 58, one rat). The count was more than three times higher in the box than in the cylinder and similar across subregions. [Park 2011, PLoS ONE](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0022349)
- Single-field cells dropped from 60.7% to 17.9% in CA1, from 81.8% to 29% in CA3 and from 63.9% to 8.2% in DG (cylinder to box). [Park 2011, PLoS ONE](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0022349)
- Field size: the largest field per cell grew in the box (environment effect F(1,341) = 14.61, p = 0.0002; CA1 > CA3 = DG), but the size of all fields pooled did not change (F(1,1316) = 0.01, p = 0.93). Absolute sizes appear only in Fig. 4B. [Park 2011, PLoS ONE](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0022349)
- Spacing was irregular in all three subregions. The angle between the three most prominent autocorrelogram peaks averaged about 33° (CA1 32.8°, CA3 32.7°, DG 33.2°), far from the 60° of a hexagonal grid. [Park 2011, PLoS ONE](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0022349)
- Caveats: CA3 and DG each come from one rat, and the reported error degrees of freedom (341) exceed the 214 listed cells, which the paper does not explain. [Park 2011, PLoS ONE](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0022349)

#### Rich, Liaw & Lee 2014 (rat dorsal CA1, tetrodes, 48 m novel track)
- Five male Long-Evans rats, 253 putative pyramidal cells (63, 58, 24, 57 and 51 per rat) tracked across sleep and running so that silent cells could be counted. 16-tetrode microdrives. [Rich 2014 supplement](https://www.its.caltech.edu/~jkenny/nb250c/papers/Rich-2014-supp.pdf)
- The track was a 48 m elevated linear track, 9 cm wide with 3 cm walls, laid out with turns, switchbacks and chicanes in a novel room of about 150 m² (180 m² for one rat). It was extended in four stages of 3, 7, 12 and 26 m (cumulative 3, 10, 22 and 48 m), with 3 to 5 traversals per stage, and analysis was restricted to the newly added novel section. Training mazes were under 2 m. [Rich 2014 supplement](https://www.its.caltech.edu/~jkenny/nb250c/papers/Rich-2014-supp.pdf)
- Field size was 65 ± 43 cm (mean ± SD) with in-field rate 5 ± 2 Hz. Neither was correlated with field location (ρ = 0.05 and 0.01) or with the number of other fields of the same cell (ρ = 0.05 and 0.05). The scatter in fig. S14 shows the largest fields at roughly 3.3 m (read from the figure, approximate). Mean field size as a fraction of the track is 0.65 / 48 = 1.4%, and the largest about 3.3 / 48 = 7% (my arithmetic). [Rich 2014 supplement, fig. S14](https://www.its.caltech.edu/~jkenny/nb250c/papers/Rich-2014-supp.pdf)
- The number of fields per cell was overdispersed relative to an equal-rate Poisson model and fit a negative binomial with r = 0.57, p = 0.14. This is equivalent to each cell being a Poisson process whose rate (its "propensity") is gamma distributed with shape α = 0.57 and rate β = 7.75, in units of fields per metre. Some cells had close to 30 fields. [Rich 2014 main text](https://www.its.caltech.edu/~jkenny/nb250c/papers/rich_14.pdf), [supplement fig. S9](https://www.its.caltech.edu/~jkenny/nb250c/papers/Rich-2014-supp.pdf)
- Per-animal mean fields per cell over 48 m were 3.56, 4.19, 4.33, 2.29 and 2.48. [Rich 2014 supplement, fig. S7](https://www.its.caltech.edu/~jkenny/nb250c/papers/Rich-2014-supp.pdf)
- Recruitment reached only about 65% of cells at 48 m (range 46 to 77% across rats). The gamma-Poisson model predicts the recruitment curve (a Lomax, or Pareto type II, distribution; KS = 0.05, P = 0.48), and extrapolation predicts 90% recruitment at about 500 m. Some cells silent on the track formed fields in a separate novel open field. [Rich 2014 main text](https://www.its.caltech.edu/~jkenny/nb250c/papers/rich_14.pdf)
- For cells with at least 6 fields (61/253), field locations were uniform and inter-field intervals exponential (0/61 cells differed from a Poisson process, Anderson-Darling, FDR-adjusted). Pooled cells with fewer fields also matched. Field locations of different cells were uncorrelated at bin sizes from 1 to 16 m. [Rich 2014 main text](https://www.its.caltech.edu/~jkenny/nb250c/papers/rich_14.pdf), [supplement figs. S6, S12](https://www.its.caltech.edu/~jkenny/nb250c/papers/Rich-2014-supp.pdf)
- Under the fitted model, the proportion of cells with exactly one field stays at about 5 to 10% over a wide range of environment sizes. [Rich 2014 main text, Fig. 4B](https://www.its.caltech.edu/~jkenny/nb250c/papers/rich_14.pdf)
- Pre-exploration slow-wave-sleep firing rate correlated with later field count (r = 0.45, P < 10^-13). Field counts per cell correlated across the track and an open field (Spearman ρ about 0.2), less strongly than within the track (about 0.4). Field number varied weakly with septotemporal and proximodistal position (r = -0.20 and -0.19 pooled), but not consistently within animals. [Rich 2014 main text](https://www.its.caltech.edu/~jkenny/nb250c/papers/rich_14.pdf), [supplement figs. S16, S17](https://www.its.caltech.edu/~jkenny/nb250c/papers/Rich-2014-supp.pdf)
- Rich et al. summarise prior work as follows. In typical environments (track under 5 m or arena under 1 m²), about 20 to 50% of dorsal CA1 pyramidal cells are place cells and most have one field, while in larger lab environments (up to 18 m long or 3 m²) more cells are recruited, many have multiple fields, and fields are wider. [Rich 2014 main text](https://www.its.caltech.edu/~jkenny/nb250c/papers/rich_14.pdf)

#### Eliav et al. 2021 (Egyptian fruit bat dorsal CA1, tetrodes, wireless logger, 200 m tunnel)
- The tunnel was 194 m long (185.3 m linearised between the two landing balls), 2.30 m wide and 2.35 m high, with a 140 m arm and a 54 m arm joined by a 43° turn, 13 landmarks and 5 lux light. Bats flew almost one-dimensional trajectories at about 8 m/s with speed CV 0.042, averaging 14.1 km per session. Only flights longer than 100 m were analysed. [Eliav 2021 PDF](https://www.weizmann.ac.il/brain-sciences/labs/ulanovsky/sites/brain-sciences.labs.ulanovsky/files/2024-11/Eliav2021.pdf)
- Dataset 1 had 5 wild-born bats and 235 putative pyramidal cells, all active in flight. 196 (83.4%) were place cells, giving 331 cell × direction maps and 1,629 fields. [Eliav 2021 PDF, Table S1](https://www.weizmann.ac.il/brain-sciences/labs/ulanovsky/sites/brain-sciences.labs.ulanovsky/files/2024-11/Eliav2021.pdf)
- Mean number of fields per direction was 4.9. Some cells had more than 10, and the histogram has a bin for 20 or more per direction. Only 12.2% of neurons had a single field overall, and those fields averaged 5.9 ± 3.5 m. [Eliav 2021 PDF](https://www.weizmann.ac.il/brain-sciences/labs/ulanovsky/sites/brain-sciences.labs.ulanovsky/files/2024-11/Eliav2021.pdf)
- Field size ranged from 0.6 to 32 m, often above 10 m. The distribution was skewed and fit a log-normal (μ = 1.57, σ = 0.575 in natural-log metres; KS P = 0.74) better than a gamma (shape 3.16, scale 1.8 m; KS P = 0.00036). The log-normal implies a median of e^1.57 = 4.8 m and a mean of about 5.7 m (my arithmetic, matching Fig. 4F). [Eliav 2021 PDF, fig. S8](https://www.weizmann.ac.il/brain-sciences/labs/ulanovsky/sites/brain-sciences.labs.ulanovsky/files/2024-11/Eliav2021.pdf)
- Within a cell, the largest-to-smallest field ratio reached 20 with a mean of 4.4 (n = 172 cells with at least 2 fields). Every individual bat had fields from about 1 m up to at least 15 m and ratios up to at least 10. Both flight directions showed similar field number (ρ = 0.71), median field size (ρ = 0.41) and size ratio (ρ = 0.33), which the authors read as a per-neuron firing propensity. [Eliav 2021 PDF, figs. S9, S10](https://www.weizmann.ac.il/brain-sciences/labs/ulanovsky/sites/brain-sciences.labs.ulanovsky/files/2024-11/Eliav2021.pdf)
- Total coverage (sum of field sizes per direction, normalised by the usable track) averaged 0.15 of the tunnel and was roughly exponentially distributed across cells. [Eliav 2021 PDF, Methods and fig. S17F](https://www.weizmann.ac.il/brain-sciences/labs/ulanovsky/sites/brain-sciences.labs.ulanovsky/files/2024-11/Eliav2021.pdf)
- Field locations were uniform along the tunnel, gaps between fields were exponentially distributed, and firing-rate maps had no spectral peaks (no spatial periodicity) in pooled data or in any bat. [Eliav 2021 PDF, Fig. 3 and figs. S20I, S22H](https://www.weizmann.ac.il/brain-sciences/labs/ulanovsky/sites/brain-sciences.labs.ulanovsky/files/2024-11/Eliav2021.pdf)
- Field peak rate correlated positively with field size (ρ = 0.29, n = 1,629 fields). In-field rate was 5.01 ± 0.27 Hz and out-of-field rate 0.24 ± 0.03 Hz. [Eliav 2021 PDF, fig. S6](https://www.weizmann.ac.il/brain-sciences/labs/ulanovsky/sites/brain-sciences.labs.ulanovsky/files/2024-11/Eliav2021.pdf)
- In a 6 m segment of the same tunnel (dataset 2, 3 bats), only 36 of 67 cells (53.7%) were active in flight and 30 were place cells. Cells had one or two fields, field sizes were much smaller, and the largest/smallest ratio was much lower (all P < 10^-5). A gamma fit to the 6 m field sizes gave shape 3.56 and scale 0.37 m (mean 1.32 m, my arithmetic). [Eliav 2021 PDF, Fig. 4 and Methods](https://www.weizmann.ac.il/brain-sciences/labs/ulanovsky/sites/brain-sciences.labs.ulanovsky/files/2024-11/Eliav2021.pdf)
- A 130 m portion of the tunnel (dataset 3, 2 bats, 125 place cells) gave somewhat fewer fields, smaller fields and smaller ratios than the 200 m tunnel, an intermediate point between 6 m and 200 m. [Eliav 2021 PDF, Fig. 5 insets](https://www.weizmann.ac.il/brain-sciences/labs/ulanovsky/sites/brain-sciences.labs.ulanovsky/files/2024-11/Eliav2021.pdf)
- From the 6 m and 200 m data, the authors fitted mean field size as scaling with environment length as L^0.36 and mean coverage as L^-0.24 (average exponent δ = 0.3, used in their simulations). [Eliav 2021 PDF, supplementary Methods](https://www.weizmann.ac.il/brain-sciences/labs/ulanovsky/sites/brain-sciences.labs.ulanovsky/files/2024-11/Eliav2021.pdf)
- The authors state that before their study the largest single-compartment environment with place-cell recordings was under 10 m, because the 48 m track of Rich et al. was a zigzag of about 1 m segments and the 18 m track of Kjelstrup et al. passed through several small rooms. [Eliav 2021 PDF](https://www.weizmann.ac.il/brain-sciences/labs/ulanovsky/sites/brain-sciences.labs.ulanovsky/files/2024-11/Eliav2021.pdf)

#### Harland et al. 2021 (rat dorsal CA1, tetrodes, wireless, 18.6 m² "megaspace")
- The megaspace was 5.3 × 3.5 m (18.55 to 18.6 m²) with 51 cm walls, flags and floor cues. Comparison arenas sharing the same floor were "very small" 90 × 60 cm (0.54 m²), "small" 180 × 120 cm (2.16 m²) and "large" 350 × 235 cm (8.2 m²). Rats were five male Brown Norway rats, and coverage was improved by having rats follow a food-baited robot. [Harland 2021 accepted manuscript](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10294326)
- 539 dorsal CA1 place cells were recorded over 54 Small-Mega-Small sessions. Analysis kept 383 cells active in all three epochs with stable small-arena fields. [Harland 2021 accepted manuscript](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10294326)
- 82% of cells had 2 to 5 subfields in the megaspace, versus 1 to 2 subfields in 91% of cells in the 2.16 m² arena. In the very small 0.54 m² arena, 94% had a single field. In the three-stage sessions, the modal count was 1 to 2 (small), 3 (large) and 4 to 10 (megaspace). Across the four arena sizes the number of subfields per cell increased linearly with area (R² = 0.9776). [Harland 2021 accepted manuscript](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10294326)
- Megaspace subfield sizes followed a negative exponential (r = 0.995), with 78% of fields at 1 m² or less and 22% between 1 and 4 m². In the small arena the distribution was closer to Gaussian (r = 0.985). In the well-isolated subsample (125 cells, 532 megaspace subfields), sizes ran from 0.023 m² up to 3.46 to 3.47 m². [Harland 2021 accepted manuscript, Fig. 3 legend and Methods](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10294326)
- Cells with more subfields had smaller subfields (negative exponential fit, r = 0.86), but the summed area per cell did not depend on subfield count (linear fit, r = 0.027). Summed area per cell was 2.15 ± 0.47 m² in robot-following sessions and 1.99 ± 0.44 m² in foraging sessions, which is 11.6% and 10.7% of 18.55 m² (my arithmetic). [Harland 2021 accepted manuscript](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10294326)
- 79% of multi-field cells had a range of subfield areas (largest minus smallest) above 0.6 m². Within-cell peak-rate differences between subfields were small. [Harland 2021 accepted manuscript](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10294326)
- Subfield configurations were irregular, with average inter-field distances normally distributed (KS D(345) = 0.044, P > 0.2), no clusters or repeated positional patterns, and a small accumulation of fields at walls and corners. [Harland 2021 accepted manuscript](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10294326)
- Summed subfield area per cell increased with arena size (R² = 0.9801, strong exponential fit r = 0.996, matching the area increase), while the fraction of the arena covered per cell was similar for the 8.2 m² and 18.6 m² arenas (Tukey P = 0.92). Relative coverage per cell was only about 2% higher in the megaspace than in the 8.8× smaller small arena, and 0.9% higher when speed was matched. [Harland 2021 accepted manuscript](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10294326)
- Mean firing rate in the megaspace was 0.93 ± 0.64 Hz and did not differ between arena sizes (P = 0.15). [Harland 2021 accepted manuscript](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10294326)

#### Tanni, de Cothi & Barry 2022 (rat dorsal CA1, tetrodes, 128 channels)
- Five male Lister Hooded rats and 629 CA1 place cells (89 to 172 per rat). Four arenas with 60 cm walls were A 87.5 × 125 cm (1.09 m²), B 175 × 125 cm (2.19 m²), C 175 × 250 cm (4.38 m²) and D 350 × 250 cm (8.75 m²). Recording time scaled with area from 15 min (A) to 120 min (D). [Tanni 2022, Europe PMC full text](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC9616721/fullTextXML)
- Fields per cell rose from 1.28 (A) to 2.28 (D), and 84.8% of cells had multiple fields counting all environments together. The normalised field count rose linearly with area (r = 0.983, slope 0.038, intercept 0.093, n = 20), as did summed field area (r = 0.996). [Tanni 2022, Europe PMC full text](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC9616721/fullTextXML)
- A gamma-Poisson model of field counts fit better than an equal-rate Poisson model (log-likelihood -1,538 vs -1,679; BIC 3,089 vs 3,365). The fitted model predicts that 99% of CA1 place cells would have at least one field in an environment larger than 51.8 m². Propensities were conserved across environments. The fitted gamma parameters were not visible in the text I could read. [Tanni 2022, Europe PMC full text](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC9616721/fullTextXML)
- Population statistics were constant across location and environment, at about 15% of cells active above 1 Hz per location and 0.52 Hz mean rate per place cell. [Tanni 2022, Europe PMC full text](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC9616721/fullTextXML)

#### Davidson, Kloosterman & Wilson 2009 (rat dorsal CA1, tetrodes)
- The track was 10.3 m long (not 10.5 m), with reward at both ends. Five rats were implanted and four analysed, with 47, 34, 23 and 32 spatially tuned units. Tuning curves used 10 cm bins smoothed with a 5 cm SD, excluding putative interneurons (> 5 Hz) and units with peak rate below 3 Hz. [Davidson 2009, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4364032/)
- The paper does not define place fields or report field counts or widths. It mentions cells with multiple firing fields and "irregularly-shaped place fields" only qualitatively. Its focus is replay, which ran at about 8 m/s of virtual speed. [Davidson 2009, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4364032/), [Europe PMC record](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=DOI:10.1016/j.neuron.2009.07.027&format=json&resultType=core)

#### Studies found through citing papers
- Lee JS, Briguglio JJ, Cohen JD, Romani S, Lee AK (2020). The statistical structure of the hippocampal code for space as a function of time, context, and value. Cell 183(3):620-635. doi:10.1016/j.cell.2020.09.024. Mice, longitudinal calcium imaging, several large virtual environments. Each cell's field propensity sets its number of fields per unit space, is preserved across environments and over months, predicts reward-related activity, is broadly distributed with many low-propensity and some very high-propensity cells, varies about 10-fold between adjacent cells, and relates to excitability in intracellular recordings. I could read only the abstract, so track lengths and counts are not verified. [PubMed 33035454](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33035454/)
- Mainali N, Azeredo da Silveira R, Burak Y (2025). Universal statistics of hippocampal place fields across species and dimensionalities. Neuron 113(7):1110-1120.e3. doi:10.1016/j.neuron.2025.01.017. Theory paper, preprint bioRxiv 2024 (doi:10.1101/2024.06.11.597569). It models fields as excursions of a thresholded random Gaussian process with two parameters (correlation length σ and normalised threshold θ) and tests it against bats and rodents in 1D, 2D and 3D, in small and large environments. [Europe PMC record](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=%22Universal%20statistics%20of%20hippocampal%20place%20fields%22&format=json&resultType=core)
- According to a transcription of the Mainali preprint (not checked against the published text), the datasets were bats in the 200 m and 6 m tunnels (Eliav), mice on a 40 m virtual track (source not identified), rats on the 48 m maze (Rich), rats in the 18.6 m² megaspace (Harland, 20 cells) and bats in a 5.8 × 4.6 × 2.7 m room. The predicted field-size distribution is P(s) = (2β/D) s^(2/D-1) exp(-β s^(2/D)), which is Rayleigh in 1D and exponential in 2D. Gaps between fields are exponential. Fitted σ grows sublinearly and θ falls with environment size, so fields per cell rise and field size grows sublinearly, while fields per unit area ("propensity") varies only weakly. The 1D model was reported to fit log field sizes better than a log-normal. [Blog transcription of the preprint](https://muatyz.github.io/posts/read/attractors/universal-statistics-of-hippocampal-place-fields-across-species-and-dimensionalities/)
- A Science Perspective by Wood and Dudchenko accompanying Eliav et al. suggests that multiscale coding may be the rule in large continuous spaces and asks whether it appears only in large enough environments. I saw this only in a search summary. [Wood & Dudchenko 2021 perspective, Weizmann copy](https://www.weizmann.ac.il/neurobiology/labs/ulanovsky/sites/neurobiology.labs.ulanovsky/files/uploads/wood_dudchenko_perspective_science2021.pdf)

### Inferences
- Fields per unit length in 1D differ by about threefold between rats and bats. Rich's fitted propensity has mean α/β = 0.57 / 7.75 = 0.074 fields/m across all cells, including silent ones (the negative binomial mean r(1-p)/p = 3.5 fields per 48 m agrees). Eliav's 4.9 fields per direction over about 185 m is about 0.026 fields/m among place cells only. Bat fields are about 9× longer (mean 5.7 m vs 0.65 m), so per-cell coverage is higher in bats (15%) than in rats (3.5 × 0.65 / 48 = 4.7%, my estimate). Species, flight vs running, speed (about 8 m/s vs under 1 m/s), familiarity and field definitions all differ, so the comparison is descriptive.
- The relative spread of field sizes is similar in the two 1D datasets. Rich's CV is 43 / 65 = 0.66 and Eliav's log-normal σ = 0.575 implies CV = sqrt(e^(0.575²) - 1) = 0.63. What differs is absolute scale and the within-cell size ratios that Eliav reports. Rich did not report within-cell ratios, so whether the rat data are also multiscale within cells cannot be judged from the paper.
- Under Rich's fitted gamma-Poisson parameters, the predicted fraction of cells with no fields on 48 m is p^r = 0.14^0.57 = 0.33 (observed about 0.35 silent), and the predicted fraction with exactly one field is r(1-p)p^r = 0.16 (my arithmetic).
- Field counts in the 2 to 2.5 m² boxes of Fenton and Park (3.4 to 5.9 per cell) are higher than in Tanni's 8.75 m² arena (2.28) and similar to Harland's 18.6 m² megaspace (2 to 5 in 82% of cells). The most likely reason is that Fenton and Park count any contiguous region of 9 or more pixels with nonzero rate as a field, while Tanni and Harland use rate thresholds. Field count per area is therefore not comparable across labs without reanalysis.
- Across all studies, absolute field size grows sublinearly with environment size and the fraction covered by each field falls. Fenton (fields 30% larger for 5.8× area), Eliav (L^0.36), Harland (larger mean subfield and summed area, flat per-cell coverage fraction between 8.2 and 18.6 m²) and the Mainali theory all agree on the direction. Park is the exception for pooled field size.

### Gaps
- Park et al. 2011 and Tanni et al. 2022 give absolute field sizes only in figures, and I could not extract them.
- Davidson et al. 2009 reports no field counts or sizes for its 10.3 m track.
- Track lengths, field counts and field sizes from Lee et al. 2020 (mice, VR) could not be verified because only the abstract was accessible.
- The Mainali et al. 2025 parameter values and dataset list come from an unofficial transcription of the preprint, and the published paper may differ.
- I found no published CA3, DG, subiculum, entorhinal or lateral septum recordings in environments as large as the 48 m track, the 200 m tunnel or the 18.6 m² megaspace. Eliav et al. explicitly list CA3 and MEC recordings in very large environments as untested.
- I found no human, non-human primate or bird single-unit data on field size or count in very large environments.
- Harland et al. cite an SfN abstract (Henriksen, Moser & Moser 2009, "Megaspace recordings from hippocampal place cells and entorhinal grid cells") that does not appear to have been published as a paper. I did not find its data.

## Q2. The largest place field reported, in absolute size and as a fraction of the environment, and evidence for fields spanning a third or more of an environment

### Takeaway
The largest single field in the sources I read is 32 m, from bat dorsal CA1 in a 200 m tunnel, which is about 17% of the tunnel. In large environments, dorsal fields are absolutely large but cover at most about 17 to 19% of the space (Eliav, Harland). Fields covering a third or more of an environment are reported in two situations: small arenas, where one field routinely covers a quarter to a third of the floor, and the ventral hippocampus on an 18 m track, where mean field length is about 5.5 m and the scale reaches about 10 m (a third to over half of the track; secondary figures, not verified against the paper).

### Cited findings
- Bat dorsal CA1, 200 m tunnel (185.3 m between landing balls). Field sizes reached 32 m, with many fields above 10 m. Fig. 2A cell 5 had a largest field of 31.3 m and cell 1 one of 20.0 m. As a fraction, 32 / 185.3 = 17.3% of the flight path (my arithmetic). [Eliav 2021 PDF](https://www.weizmann.ac.il/brain-sciences/labs/ulanovsky/sites/brain-sciences.labs.ulanovsky/files/2024-11/Eliav2021.pdf)
- In the same dataset, a cell's total coverage (sum over fields per direction) averaged 15% of the tunnel. The Fig. 2D histogram extends to about 150 m of coverage, about 80% of the tunnel on its top axis (read from the figure, approximate), achieved through many fields rather than one. [Eliav 2021 PDF, Fig. 2D](https://www.weizmann.ac.il/brain-sciences/labs/ulanovsky/sites/brain-sciences.labs.ulanovsky/files/2024-11/Eliav2021.pdf)
- Eliav et al. considered whether small environments hide very large fields that cover the whole space, a "pinhole view" of a multiscale map. They argued against it because the fraction of active cells that were place cells was the same in the 6 m and 200 m tunnels (83.3% vs 83.4%), whereas cells firing over the whole small tunnel would lower it. [Eliav 2021 PDF, Discussion](https://www.weizmann.ac.il/brain-sciences/labs/ulanovsky/sites/brain-sciences.labs.ulanovsky/files/2024-11/Eliav2021.pdf)
- Bat 6 m tunnel. The gamma fit to field sizes has a mean of 3.56 × 0.37 m = 1.32 m, which is 22% of the 6 m tunnel (my arithmetic). [Eliav 2021 PDF, supplementary Methods](https://www.weizmann.ac.il/brain-sciences/labs/ulanovsky/sites/brain-sciences.labs.ulanovsky/files/2024-11/Eliav2021.pdf)
- Rat dorsal CA1, 18.6 m² megaspace. The largest subfields fall in the 1.23 to 3.47 m² band, and 3.47 / 18.55 = 18.7% of the arena (my arithmetic). A 3.46 m² circle has a diameter of about 2.1 m. Summed area per cell was about 2.0 to 2.15 m² (about 11% of the arena). [Harland 2021 accepted manuscript](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10294326)
- Rat dorsal CA1, 48 m track. The largest fields in the field-size scatter are about 3.3 m (read from fig. S14), about 7% of the track. Mean 0.65 m is 1.4%. [Rich 2014 supplement, fig. S14](https://www.its.caltech.edu/~jkenny/nb250c/papers/Rich-2014-supp.pdf)
- Rat dorsal CA1, 68 cm cylinder vs 2.1 m² chamber. Fields covered 27% of the cylinder floor but 7.3% of the chamber floor. [Fenton 2008, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2695947/)
- Rat hippocampus along the longitudinal axis, 18 m linear track (Kjelstrup KB, Solstad T, Brun VH, Hafting T, Leutgeb S, Witter MP, Moser EI, Moser MB, 2008, Finite scale of spatial representation in the hippocampus, Science 321:140-143, doi:10.1126/science.1157086). The abstract reports that the scale of place fields increases almost linearly from under 1 m at the dorsal pole to about 10 m at the ventral pole, and that place fields were well defined at all levels. [Europe PMC record with abstract](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=DOI:10.1126/science.1157086&format=json&resultType=core)
- Secondary sources quote Kjelstrup's mean field sizes as 0.98 ± 0.03 m (dorsal), 3.73 ± 0.43 m (intermediate) and 5.52 ± 0.54 m (ventral), and mention phase precession in ventral CA3 extending over distances as large as 10 m. These numbers come from an aggregator page, not the Science text, so they are unverified. As fractions of the 18 m track, they are 5%, 21% and 31%, and a 10 m field is 56% (my arithmetic). [Bohrium aggregator page](https://www.bohrium.com/paper-details/finite-scale-of-spatial-representation-in-the-hippocampus/811915281508073473-7984)
- Rat ventral vs dorsal hippocampus in a square foraging arena (Jung MW, Wiener SI, McNaughton BL, 1994, J Neurosci 14:7347-7356, doi:10.1523/JNEUROSCI.14-12-07347.1994). Fewer ventral complex-spike cells had place fields, and ventral spatial selectivity was "of significantly lower resolution". The abstract gives no field sizes. [Jung 1994, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6576902/)
- Field-detection rules can cap the largest detectable field. Tanni et al. rejected candidate fields larger than half the rate-map bins, so fields covering more than 50% of an arena could not appear in their counts. [Tanni 2022, Europe PMC full text](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC9616721/fullTextXML)

### Inferences
- Measured as a fraction of the environment, large fields are mostly a small-environment and ventral-hippocampus phenomenon. In dorsal CA1, enlarging the environment makes fields absolutely bigger but fractionally smaller, and cells add fields instead. The largest dorsal fractions in large environments (17 to 19%) come from two independent datasets in different species and dimensions.
- No source I read reports a dorsal hippocampal field covering a third or more of an environment larger than about 6 m or 2 m². The candidates for fields that large are ventral recordings on long tracks (Kjelstrup) and per-cell total coverage summed over many fields (Eliav), which is a different quantity from single-field size.
- Rules that merge peaks (Eliav merges adjacent peaks whose dip stays above 50% of the larger peak), cap field size (Tanni) or use cell-specific thresholds (Harland) all change the maximum. The "largest field" figures above are therefore conditional on each paper's definition.

### Gaps
- I could not access the Kjelstrup et al. 2008 full text to confirm the mean field sizes, the recording subfield (secondary sources point to CA3), the number of cells, the field definition or the largest single ventral field.
- I found no study reporting single fields covering a third or more of a large (many-metre) environment in dorsal hippocampus, and none in CA2, subiculum, lateral septum or entorhinal cortex in large environments. This is a negative result of my search, not proof of absence.
- The Eliav coverage maximum (about 150 m, about 80%) is read off a histogram axis and is approximate.

## Q3. Classic and later scaling experiments: do fields scale with the environment, stay fixed, or both?

### Takeaway
Individual cells respond in mixed ways when a familiar space is resized. Some cells keep the same relative position and scale, others remap, and fields tend to stay a fixed distance from nearby walls, stretching or splitting when walls move apart. Quantitatively, field size grows with environment size but less than proportionally, and the extra space is covered mainly by more fields and more active cells. This produces a mix of fixed-scale and scaling behaviour, and in very large spaces a range of field sizes within a single cell.

### Cited findings
- Muller RU, Kubie JL (1987). The effects of changes in the environment on the spatial firing of hippocampal complex-spike cells. J Neurosci 7(7):1951-1968. doi:10.1523/JNEUROSCI.07-07-01951.1987. The standard 76 cm diameter, 51 cm high cylinder was scaled up by a factor of 2 in diameter and height (to about 152 cm, 4× the floor area). Of cells recorded in both cylinders, 36% had fields that "also scaled" (same angular and relative radial position), 52% had very different firing patterns (remapped) and 12% were virtually silent in both. Similar results held for small and large rectangles, while changing a circle to a rectangle made firing unpredictable. [Muller & Kubie 1987, PMC abstract](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6568940/), [Europe PMC record](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=DOI:10.1523/JNEUROSCI.07-07-01951.1987&format=json&resultType=core)
- O'Keefe J, Burgess N (1996). Geometric determinants of the place fields of hippocampal neurons. Nature 381:425-428. doi:10.1038/381425a0. The same cells were recorded in four rectangular boxes differing only in the length of one or both sides. Most results were explained by a model in which a field is the thresholded sum of Gaussian tuning curves, each oriented perpendicular to a box wall and peaked at a fixed distance from it. [Europe PMC record with abstract](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=DOI:10.1038/381425a0&format=json&resultType=core)
- A later paper summarises O'Keefe & Burgess as showing that stretching a familiar rectangle along one axis stretches place fields along the same axis. [Search result summary of a Durham repository paper](https://durham-repository.worktribe.com/OutputFile/1480792)
- This model became the boundary vector cell model (Hartley T, Burgess N, Lever C, Cacucci F, O'Keefe J, 2000, Hippocampus 10:369-379), which predicts an extra field when a barrier is inserted. Boundary vector cells were later found in subiculum and entorhinal cortex. [Burgess & O'Keefe 2011 review, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3223517/)
- Linear track shortening (Diba K, Buzsáki G, 2008, J Neurosci 28(50):13448-13456, doi:10.1523/JNEUROSCI.3824-08.2008). Rat CA1 and CA3, 3 rats. The track was shortened from 170 to 100 cm (-41%). Among the 1,147 of 1,750 fields that persisted, mean field size (extent above 2 Hz) fell from 52 to 42 cm (-19%, rank-sum p < 10^-12) and field overlap increased. As a fraction of track, a field went from 31% (52/170) to 42% (42/100), my arithmetic. [Diba & Buzsáki 2008, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2656938/)
- Enlarging a 2D arena 5.8× made CA1 fields 30% larger on average (66.2 vs 57.8 pixels; 94.6 vs 52.4 pixels for the same cells on the floor), while fields per cell rose from 1.3 to 3.4. [Fenton 2008, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2695947/)
- Enlarging 5.6× (CA1, CA3, DG) increased the largest field per cell but not pooled field size, while fields per cell rose more than threefold. Harland et al. note that this result differs from earlier reports of larger mean fields. [Park 2011, PLoS ONE](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0022349), [Harland 2021 accepted manuscript](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10294326)
- Across 0.54, 2.16, 8.2 and 18.6 m², subfield count rose linearly with area (R² = 0.978). Summed area per cell rose with area (R² = 0.980) and per-cell coverage fraction plateaued between 8.2 and 18.6 m², while the variability of subfield sizes grew with arena size. Subfield locations in the megaspace were not correlated with locations in the embedded small arena. [Harland 2021 accepted manuscript](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10294326)
- Over 1.09 to 8.75 m², field count and summed field area grew linearly with area (r = 0.983 and 0.996), and per-cell propensities were conserved across environments. [Tanni 2022, Europe PMC full text](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC9616721/fullTextXML)
- Bats in 6, 130 and 200 m tunnels showed increasing fields per direction, field size and within-cell size ratio with tunnel length. Fitted exponents were field size ∝ L^0.36 and coverage ∝ L^-0.24. [Eliav 2021 PDF, Fig. 5 insets and supplementary Methods](https://www.weizmann.ac.il/brain-sciences/labs/ulanovsky/sites/brain-sciences.labs.ulanovsky/files/2024-11/Eliav2021.pdf)
- On a progressively extended 48 m track, new track sections produced new fields in already active cells and recruited previously silent cells. Field propensity was stationary along the track and did not change at section boundaries (across-section vs within-section Spearman ρ, for example 0.50 vs 0.38, p = 0.07). [Rich 2014 main text](https://www.its.caltech.edu/~jkenny/nb250c/papers/rich_14.pdf), [supplement fig. S11](https://www.its.caltech.edu/~jkenny/nb250c/papers/Rich-2014-supp.pdf)
- Theory. In the Mainali et al. thresholded Gaussian-process account, the fitted correlation length grows sublinearly and the threshold falls with environment size, so fields per cell rise and field size grows sublinearly. This summary is from a transcription of the preprint. [Blog transcription of the preprint](https://muatyz.github.io/posts/read/attractors/universal-statistics-of-hippocampal-place-fields-across-species-and-dimensionalities/)
- Theory. Harland et al. contrast their data with the "megamap" attractor model (Hedrick K, Zhang K, 2016, J Neurophysiol 116:868-891), in which cells have multiple subfields of similar size that can enlarge to fill any space. Harland et al. report subfields of different sizes instead. [Harland 2021 accepted manuscript](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10294326)

### Inferences
- The answer to "scale, stay fixed, or both" is both, at two levels. Across cells, a minority rescale while most remap when a familiar arena is doubled (Muller & Kubie, 36% vs 52%). Within a remapped or novel large space, mean field size grows sublinearly. Exponents implied by the data include about 0.36 for 1D length in bats, 30% for 5.8× area in rats (about 0.15 in area terms, my arithmetic), and 19% shrinkage for 41% shortening (about 0.4 in length terms, my arithmetic). Field count absorbs most of the extra space.
- O'Keefe & Burgess's boundary-anchored fields, which keep a fixed distance to a wall and therefore stretch or split when walls move apart, offer one mechanism by which fields neither fully scale nor stay fixed. Eliav et al.'s finding that field size in the tunnel was unrelated to distance from landmarks argues that the bat multiscale code is not just landmark anchoring.

### Gaps
- I could not access the O'Keefe & Burgess 1996 full text. Box dimensions, cell counts, the proportion of fields that stretched, and how often fields split into two peaks in the largest box are all unverified. From prior knowledge only, the boxes are usually described as 61 × 61, 61 × 122, 122 × 61 and 122 × 122 cm with some fields becoming bimodal in the large square box, but I did not confirm this from the source.
- Muller & Kubie's abstract does not say whether field area quadrupled with the 4× floor area or grew less, or give cell counts.
- I found no study that systematically varied linear-track length over a wide range (for example 1 to 50 m) in the same animals and measured field width at each length. Diba & Buzsáki (1.7 to 1.0 m) and Eliav (6, 130 and 200 m in different bats) are the closest.
- I found no replication of the O'Keefe & Burgess stretching experiment at the scale of tens of metres.

## Q4. Do field size and field count depend on position (centre vs edge, landmarks, goals), running speed or familiarity in large environments?

### Takeaway
Position matters in 2D arenas. Fields near walls are smaller and more numerous, and fields toward the centre are larger and sparser (Tanni, Harland). Along 1D tracks and tunnels, field density and size are uniform except at reward sites, where fields are denser and smaller (bats). Running or flight speed did not explain field size in the two large 1D datasets. Familiarity changes field stability more than field statistics: the bat multiscale code was present from day 1 and in lab-born bats, while rat megaspace maps were less stable across visits than small-arena maps.

### Cited findings
- Position, 2D arenas up to 8.75 m². Field peak density in 25 cm bands was highest near walls and fell toward the centre (environment D, H = 16.1, p = 0.001). Field area increased with distance from the wall (D, H = 10.5, p = 0.015), mainly through width orthogonal to the nearest wall (H = 12.1, p = 0.007) rather than width parallel to it (H = 0.39, p = 0.94). Density in the band nearest the wall was higher in smaller environments. Proportion of active cells (about 15%) and mean rate (0.52 Hz) were constant across wall distance. [Tanni 2022, Europe PMC full text](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC9616721/fullTextXML)
- Tanni et al. found that the rate of change of the place-cell population correlated with the rate of change of the visual scene in a VR replica of the largest arena (r = 0.60, partial r = 0.51 controlling for behaviour). Correlation with running speed was r = -0.30, with turning rate r = 0.27 and with a path-integration measure r = -0.06. [Tanni 2022, Europe PMC full text](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC9616721/fullTextXML)
- Position, 18.6 m² megaspace. There was a small accumulation of fields near walls. Subfield size correlated moderately with distance to the nearest wall (r = 0.36), less than in the small arena (r = 0.40). Proportions of corner, wall and middle subfields were similar across scales. [Harland 2021 accepted manuscript](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10294326)
- Position, 48 m track. Overall field density was uniform along the track (KS = 0.03, P = 0.36) and showed no consistent dependence on track angle relative to the room, distance to the nearest track corner, or running speed in 5 cm bins (shuffle tests). [Rich 2014 main text](https://www.its.caltech.edu/~jkenny/nb250c/papers/rich_14.pdf), [supplement fig. S10](https://www.its.caltech.edu/~jkenny/nb250c/papers/Rich-2014-supp.pdf)
- Rich et al. note that the gamma-Poisson model did not need goal effects in their task, but cite other work showing higher field density around goals (Hollup et al. 2001) and changing recruitment over time (Karlsson & Frank 2008; Ziv et al. 2013). [Rich 2014 main text](https://www.its.caltech.edu/~jkenny/nb250c/papers/rich_14.pdf)
- Position, 200 m tunnel. Field peaks were distributed uniformly. Distances to the nearest of 13 landmarks matched shuffles (KS P = 0.82 and 0.18), and fields within 5 m of a landmark did not differ in size from fields farther away (KS P = 0.80 and 0.25). Field size barely related to the local inter-landmark distance (Spearman ρ = 0.04 and 0.09). The authors caution that very large fields might still occur in regions with no spatial information over long distances. [Eliav 2021 PDF, Fig. 3 and fig. S15](https://www.weizmann.ac.il/brain-sciences/labs/ulanovsky/sites/brain-sciences.labs.ulanovsky/files/2024-11/Eliav2021.pdf)
- Reward sites, 200 m tunnel. Within 4 m of the food-baited landing balls (zones excluded from the main analysis because of low speed), fields were denser (z = 11.46 for the bin next to the ball) and smaller (field size vs distance to ball ρ = 0.64, P = 4 × 10^-19). A few fields over 10 m still peaked within 4 m of a ball. The authors list reward salience, low speed or boundary-vector effects as possible causes. [Eliav 2021 PDF, fig. S14](https://www.weizmann.ac.il/brain-sciences/labs/ulanovsky/sites/brain-sciences.labs.ulanovsky/files/2024-11/Eliav2021.pdf)
- Speed, 200 m tunnel. Flight speed was nearly constant (mean CV 0.042). The within-cell largest/smallest field ratio did not correlate with the speed ratio at those fields (ρ = 0.03, P = 0.67), and the speed ratio was centred on 1 (SD 0.10). [Eliav 2021 PDF](https://www.weizmann.ac.il/brain-sciences/labs/ulanovsky/sites/brain-sciences.labs.ulanovsky/files/2024-11/Eliav2021.pdf)
- Speed, megaspace. Robot-following sessions had higher speed and slightly larger summed subfield area per cell than foraging (2.15 vs 1.99 m², F(1,381) = 10.57, P < 0.01). The difference disappeared in 6 velocity-matched sessions per condition. Subfield number and mean subfield size did not differ between behaviours. [Harland 2021 accepted manuscript](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10294326)
- Familiarity, bats. Recordings in a 130 m tunnel from the first day of exposure showed multifield, multiscale cells on day 1, with no trend over more than 30 days in fields per direction (ρ = 0.02, P = 0.81), field size (ρ = 0.06, P = 0.13) or size ratio (ρ = -0.04, P = 0.75). Fields appeared and disappeared within sessions more often on days 1 and 2 (two-proportion z test, P < 0.001) but also later. [Eliav 2021 PDF, Fig. 5](https://www.weizmann.ac.il/brain-sciences/labs/ulanovsky/sites/brain-sciences.labs.ulanovsky/files/2024-11/Eliav2021.pdf)
- Developmental experience, bats. Lab-born bats raised in a 5.3 × 5.0 × 2.9 m colony room (95 place cells, 3 bats) showed the same multiscale code. Fields per direction (P = 0.18) and within-cell size ratio (P = 0.64) did not differ from wild-born bats, while wild-born bats had slightly larger fields (P = 5 × 10^-19). [Eliav 2021 PDF, Fig. 6](https://www.weizmann.ac.il/brain-sciences/labs/ulanovsky/sites/brain-sciences.labs.ulanovsky/files/2024-11/Eliav2021.pdf)
- Familiarity, rats in the megaspace. Maps were less stable between two megaspace visits than between two small-arena visits (t(920) = 8.33, P < 0.0001). Subfield number and mean subfield area did not differ between visits, but summed subfield area was larger on the second visit (P < 0.05). [Harland 2021 accepted manuscript](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10294326)
- Novelty, rats on the 48 m track. All statistics come from the first 3 to 5 traversals of entirely novel track in a novel room. Fields that were stable over the first and second halves of passes made up about 82 to 100% of fields depending on the in-field rate cutoff. [Rich 2014 supplement, figs. S2, S15](https://www.its.caltech.edu/~jkenny/nb250c/papers/Rich-2014-supp.pdf)

### Inferences
- The 2D wall effect (smaller, denser fields at boundaries) and the 1D reward effect (smaller, denser fields at landing balls) both mean that field statistics measured in the middle of a large space differ from those near its edges or goals. Eliav and Rich excluded or found no structure at landmarks, so their uniformity results apply to the interior of linear environments with weak local cues.
- Harland and Rich disagree on whether novelty explains the difference between their results. Harland argues that Rich's novel-only, 1D, about 4× smaller floor area may hide multi-scale structure. Eliav's day-1 data suggest that novelty alone does not abolish multiscale coding in bats.

### Gaps
- I found no large-environment study that tested field size against running speed in 2D, beyond Harland's behaviour comparison.
- Goal-related field accumulation (for example Hollup et al. 2001) has not, as far as I found, been quantified in environments of tens of metres in rodents.
- I did not find Karlsson & Frank 2008 or other novelty studies that measured field size in large environments, so whether novel large-environment fields are wider than familiar ones is unresolved here.

## Q5. How the studies define a field, and how sensitive the large-field and multi-field results are to those definitions

### Takeaway
Definitions differ widely: any nonzero firing (Fenton, Park), fixed 2 Hz thresholds (Rich), cell-specific SD thresholds (Harland), percentage-of-peak boundaries with spike-percentile extents (Eliav), and iterative thresholds with a maximum-size cap (Tanni). The headline results were stable within each study across the variants its authors tested. The gamma-Poisson count distribution held at higher thresholds (Rich), the bat multiscale distributions held across nine detection variants (Eliav), and the megaspace size range held across bin sizes and thresholds (Harland). Absolute counts and sizes are not comparable across labs.

### Cited findings
- Fenton 2008. A field is at least 9 contiguous 4.7 cm pixels with rate > 0 AP/s, unsmoothed, visited pixels only. Place cells needed coherence > 0.25 and information > 0.5 bits/AP. The authors note that one conventional field may contain several overlapping fields, so counts may be underestimates. [Fenton 2008, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2695947/)
- Park 2011. A field is at least 9 contiguous 2.8 cm pixels with rate > 0 AP/s (minimum about 70.6 cm²). The active subset is cells above 0.1 AP/s. [Park 2011, PLoS ONE](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0022349)
- Rich 2014. Directional linearised maps use 1 cm bins, a Gaussian with 10 cm SD, speed > 5 cm/s and occupancy > 0.001 s/cm. A field is at least 15 contiguous cm above 2 Hz, bounded where rate drops below 2 Hz, with at least 1 spike on at least 50% of passes. Directional fields were merged if either overlapped the other by at least 50%. The open field used fields of at least 100 cm² above 2 Hz with 5 cm smoothing. Cells without fields fired at 0.006 ± 0.01 Hz. [Rich 2014 supplement](https://www.its.caltech.edu/~jkenny/nb250c/papers/Rich-2014-supp.pdf)
- Rich 2014 sensitivity. About 50% of fields had in-field rate above 4.8 Hz. Raising the minimum in-field rate (to about 5 Hz, where about 92% of fields were stable across halves) still gave field counts and recruitment that matched the gamma-Poisson model. Field number did not correlate with cluster quality (L-ratio ρ = -0.01, isolation distance ρ = 0.07). [Rich 2014 supplement, figs. S3, S15](https://www.its.caltech.edu/~jkenny/nb250c/papers/Rich-2014-supp.pdf)
- Eliav 2021. Maps use 20 cm bins and a Gaussian with σ = 0.5 m (10 cm bins and σ = 2 bins in the 6 m tunnel). Fields start as peaks above 1 Hz. A lower peak was discarded when the dip between it and a larger neighbour stayed above 50% of the larger peak. The boundary is the zone above 20% of the peak, and field size is the 5th to 95th percentile of spike positions inside that zone, chosen to avoid smoothing-induced widening of small fields. Fields needed spikes on at least 5 laps or 20% of laps, local spatial information above 95% of 1,000 shuffles, and were dropped if wholly inside takeoff or landing zones. Place cells needed at least 50 spikes and spatial information above 0.25 bits/spike and above 99% of shuffles. [Eliav 2021 PDF, supplementary Methods and fig. S4](https://www.weizmann.ac.il/brain-sciences/labs/ulanovsky/sites/brain-sciences.labs.ulanovsky/files/2024-11/Eliav2021.pdf)
- Eliav 2021 sensitivity. The distributions of fields per direction, field size and size ratio were essentially unchanged across nine detection variants. The variants used 10% or 30% of peak for boundaries, 0 to 100%, 10 to 90% or 20 to 80% spike-position percentiles, 25% or 30% lap requirements, and a minimum spike count per lap. Field counts across variants ranged from 1,493 to 1,755. With the 20 to 80% percentile rule the field-size tail ends near 20 m instead of 32 m. Number of fields and size ratio did not correlate with isolation distance (ρ = 0.061 and 0.070). [Eliav 2021 PDF, figs. S11, S12](https://www.weizmann.ac.il/brain-sciences/labs/ulanovsky/sites/brain-sciences.labs.ulanovsky/files/2024-11/Eliav2021.pdf)
- Harland 2021. Bins were 12 × 12 camera pixels, stated as "5.5 cm²" for the small arenas and "12 cm²" for the megaspace (the units may mean cm per side). Speed > 10 cm/s, single-spike bins and occupancy under 0.08 s treated as empty, square Hanning smoothing, and spikes during sharp-wave ripples removed. Place cells had 0.1 to 5 Hz mean rate and spatial information above 0.5. A field was a disconnected region above 200 cm² with rate above 1.2 SD over the mean of all bins. [Harland 2021 accepted manuscript, Methods](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10294326)
- Harland 2021 sensitivity. The wider range of subfield sizes in the megaspace persisted with different bin sizes, a larger cell population, a fixed 1 Hz threshold and thresholds of 0.6, 0.9 and 1.5 SD. With the fixed 1 Hz threshold, 45 of 383 cells had no fields, the spread of summed area per cell became much wider, and summed area became slightly negatively correlated with subfield number. The small-arena size distribution became quasi-linear at lower thresholds. The authors caution that the cell-specific threshold narrows the range of summed areas across cells. [Harland 2021 accepted manuscript](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10294326)
- Tanni 2022. Maps used 4 cm bins smoothed by a Gaussian of 2 bins, with speed > 10 cm/s. Fields were found by iterative thresholding from 1 Hz in 0.05 Hz steps and needed at least 10 bins, a peak of at least 2 Hz, and odd/even-minute correlation of at least 0.25. Fields larger than half the map were rejected. Place cells needed mean rate under 4 Hz and odd/even correlation above 0.5. [Tanni 2022, Europe PMC full text](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC9616721/fullTextXML)
- Diba & Buzsáki 2008 measured field size as the extent above 2 Hz and also checked full width at half maximum in a supplementary figure. [Diba & Buzsáki 2008, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2656938/)
- Davidson 2009 did not define fields. Its units were tuned in 10 cm bins with 5 cm SD smoothing and a 3 Hz peak-rate minimum. [Davidson 2009, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4364032/)

### Inferences
- The low thresholds of Fenton and Park (any nonzero firing over 9 or more pixels) probably inflate field counts relative to threshold-based methods, and could also merge neighbouring fields when firing between them never reaches zero. Their 3.4 to 5.9 fields per cell in about 2 to 2.5 m² sit next to 2.28 per cell in 8.75 m² (Tanni). The disagreement over whether field size grows with environment size (Fenton yes, Park no for pooled fields) may also partly reflect how merging and fragmentation scale with arena size under a zero threshold.
- For the large-field question, peak-merging and upper caps matter more than the edge threshold. Eliav's dip criterion merges sub-peaks, which can enlarge fields, yet field sizes held up when the edge threshold moved between 10% and 30% of peak. Tanni's 50% cap rules out detection of very large fields. Any synthesis of "how large fields get" should list each paper's merging and capping rules alongside its numbers.
- Rate-threshold choices interact with the positive correlation between field size and peak rate in bats (ρ = 0.29). A fixed Hz threshold drops weak small fields preferentially, which would shift size distributions upward.

### Gaps
- None of the rodent large-environment papers I read report the full distribution of field sizes under alternative merging rules (for example, how often two adjacent fields merge into one large field), so the sensitivity of the very largest fields to merging is unknown.
- Fenton and Park do not report how their counts would change under a rate threshold. I found no reanalysis applying a common field definition across the Fenton, Park, Rich, Harland, Tanni and Eliav datasets. The Mainali et al. 2025 theory paper reanalysed several of them, but I could not read its methods directly.
- Harland's bin-size units ("5.5 cm²" vs "12 cm²") are ambiguous in the accepted manuscript and should be checked against the published version.
