## Gist

Place cells in small rooms usually have one smooth, bell-shaped field. In long tunnels, long tracks and large arenas they have several fields of very different sizes and shapes. This paper shows that one simple rule reproduces those irregular fields. Each CA1 cell gets its own smooth random function of position, drawn from a Gaussian process, and fires wherever that function rises above a threshold. Two numbers per dataset, the function's correlation length and the threshold, are fitted to two averages from the data, such as the mean field size and the mean gap between fields. With only those two numbers the model reproduces the full distributions of field sizes, gaps and counts, and it predicts field-shape and topology statistics that it was never fitted to. The tests use bats in 1D tunnels and a 3D room, rats on a 1D track and in 2D arenas, and mice on a 1D virtual track. In larger environments the fitted correlation length grows more slowly than the environment and the threshold falls, so cells have more fields with less regular shapes. Random synaptic weights on many spatially tuned inputs produce exactly this kind of Gaussian input, so the authors suggest that projections into CA1 are predominantly random. The evidence for that is the fit itself. The paper has no connectivity data, no population-level analysis, and no comparison with the gamma-Poisson model of field counts.

## Detailed summary

### Question and main claim

The question is whether the irregular place fields seen in large environments follow common mathematical principles across bats and rodents, and whether their statistics say anything about the synaptic wiring of CA1 (p. 1110). Earlier theory had embedded multi-field patterns in CA3 attractor models, predicted an exponential field-size distribution from a normative theory, and shown that multi-peaked, multi-scale tuning helps coding efficiency. The authors say no theory explained the heterogeneity across experimental conditions from basic principles (pp. 1110-1111).

The main claim, in the Summary (p. 1110), is that "place fields are generated by thresholding a realization of a random Gaussian process". The model captures the statistics of field arrangements and makes new quantitative predictions about field shapes and topologies. The authors verify these "in bats and rodents, in one, two, and three dimensions, in both small and large environments". They conclude that common mechanisms underlie the diverse statistics and that the results "further suggest that synaptic projections to CA1 are predominantly random". The Highlights (PDF p. 1) add that "CA1 inputs are modeled as Gaussian processes, indicative of random connectivity".

### The model

Generative process (pp. 1111, e1-e2):

- Each cell's input h(x) is an independent draw ("a realization of the process is sampled for each cell", p. 1111) from a Gaussian process over D-dimensional space with mean 0 and stationary covariance r(x − x′) (Eq. 2, p. e1). The authors set the mean to zero because it is redundant with the threshold (p. 1111).
- The firing rate is proportional to f(x) = max(h(x) − θ·√r(0), 0) (Eq. 3, p. e1). The cell therefore fires in proportion to how far its input exceeds the threshold, and is silent below it (Fig. 1A, p. 1112).
- θ is the normalized threshold, the threshold divided by the standard deviation of the process (pp. 1111, e2).
- σ is the correlation length, σ = √(−r(0)/r″(0)) (Eq. 4, p. e2). It is the ratio of the standard deviation of the process to the standard deviation of its spatial derivative.
- Simulations use a translation-invariant Gaussian correlation function (p. e2).
- A field is a connected component of nonzero activity and a gap is a connected component of zero activity (p. e2).

Analytic results (p. 1111):

- A result from the mathematics of excursion sets (Nosko 1988, ref 32) says that threshold-crossing statistics take a universal form when the correlation function is smooth enough and the threshold is high enough. They then depend only on σ and θ, not on the detailed shape of r (p. 1111).
- The Kac-Rice formula gives the mean density of fields and the mean field size in terms of σ and θ (Methods S3, Eqs. S12 and S14; supplement, not read).
- In the high-threshold limit the field-size distribution is Eq. 1, P(s) = (2β/D)·s^(2/D − 1)·exp(−β·s^(2/D)). Here s is length in 1D, area in 2D and volume in 3D, and β is a function of σ and θ (Eq. S18; supplement, not read). This is a Rayleigh distribution, 2βs·exp(−βs²), in 1D (p. 1111), an exponential, β·exp(−βs), in 2D (p. 1114), and (2/3)β·s^(−1/3)·exp(−β·s^(2/3)) in 3D (printed in Fig. 2J, p. 1113).
- The authors attribute the skew of the 1D size distribution to statistical dependence between the two adjacent threshold crossings that bound one field (p. 1111).
- In the same limit, threshold crossings are spatially uncorrelated, so gaps are exponential with a mean set by σ and θ (p. 1111), and field counts are Poisson because field locations are independent (p. 1114).
- These analytic forms are high-threshold approximations. The exact model predictions in the figures come from simulations (Fig. 1C legend, p. 1112).
- The Euler characteristic (EC) of the above-threshold set has closed forms (Eqs. 8-10, p. e3). In 1D the EC equals the number of fields (p. 1114), and Eq. 8 gives its mean as (L/σ)/(2π)·e^(−θ²/2) + (1 − Φ(θ)), where L is the environment length and Φ the Gaussian CDF. Eq. 9 (2D) is [(L/σ)²/(2π)^(3/2)·θ + 2(L/σ)/(2π)]·e^(−θ²/2) + (1 − Φ(θ)). Eq. 10 (3D) is [(L/σ)³/(2π)²·(θ² − 1) + 3(L/σ)²/(2π)^(3/2)·θ + 3(L/σ)/(2π)]·e^(−θ²/2) + (1 − Φ(θ)). The text layer garbles these, and I reconstructed them from it. Their structure matches the standard Gaussian-field EC formula for a square or cube of side L.

Free parameters:

- Two per dataset, σ and θ ("With only two parameters", p. 1117; "Each one of the experimental datasets is well explained by a single choice of the values of σ and θ", p. 1116).
- Comparisons that involve firing rates also need a linear gain factor, because the model rate is only proportional to f(x). For the size-versus-peak-rate plots the authors match the y intercept of the log-log fit between data and model (Fig. 3C legend, p. 1115; p. e3). Before computing the EC they normalize the data's mean maximum rate to the model's (p. e3).
- In the log-normal comparison the authors count the model as having one fewer degree of freedom than the two-parameter log-normal. Their reason is that fitting the gap distribution as well leaves only one free direction in parameter space (p. e2).
- An extended model gives each cell its own threshold drawn from a distribution (p. 1116; Figure S4, supplement, not read). The main text does not give the form of that distribution or its parameter count.

Why the authors chose this model (p. 1111). First, a Gaussian process is the maximum-entropy process for a given mean and covariance, so the model adds no assumptions beyond the input correlation structure. Second, summing many independent random variables gives Gaussian statistics, so the summed input to CA1 from CA3 and entorhinal cortex should be roughly Gaussian if synaptic weights are predominantly random (Fig. 1B; Methods S1 and S2, supplement, not read). Fig. 1B (p. 1112) draws this as inputs X_i from many spatially selective neurons, summed with random weights W_ji and rectified, f_j = φ(Σ_i W_ji X_i). Third, a code made of random Gaussian processes can have exponential coding efficiency (ref 29, Malerba et al. preprint).

### How the model is fitted (pp. e2-e3)

- 200 m bat tunnel. σ and θ are set by matching the mean field size and the mean gap to simulations.
- 6 m bat tunnel. Most cells had a single field, so the fit matches the mean field size and the mean fraction of the environment in which a cell was active.
- 130 m bat tunnel and 48 m rat maze. The fit matches mean field size and mean field count.
- 40 m virtual track. Only a histogram of field sizes was available, so the authors fit the Rayleigh parameter β of Eq. 1 directly.
- In 1D, parameters matched by simulation agreed well with the analytic formulas (Methods S3, Eqs. S21-S22; supplement, not read).
- 2D rat arenas. The fit matches the mean field count and mean field size reported by Harland et al. 2021. Experiments exclude cells with no fields, so the authors fit the count distribution conditioned on at least one field. For a Poisson count the conditioned mean is ⟨N⟩/(1 − e^(−⟨N⟩)) (Eq. 5).
- 3D bat room. The fit matches the mean field volume and the mean fraction of the volume in which a cell was active, from the full rate maps.

Data processing (pp. e2-e3). Bat 1D and 3D rate maps were rectified at about 0.5 Hz to remove noise. 3D maps also went through a median filter of size 2 at 10 cm resolution. 3D fields larger than the mean plus 3 standard deviations were removed as likely merges of two or more fields, including from the slice analyses. Peak counting used Gaussian smoothing with SD 0.5 to 1 m in the 200 m tunnel and 0.3 to 0.6 m in the 6 m tunnel, and dropped fields cut off at the track ends. The size-versus-peak-rate analysis rectified 1D fields at 1 Hz, and the fitted exponent did not change for higher cut-offs (Figure S5B). Boundary slopes came from a line through the two rate samples, 0.1 m apart, at each field edge. Error bars on model histograms are standard deviations over 10⁴ simulations, each matched to the number of cells in the data (p. e2).

### Main quantitative results

#### Goodness of fit

- 200 m tunnel. Matching only the means of field size and gap reproduced the full distributions of both (Figs. 1C-D, p. 1111). On field sizes the model beat the best-fit log-normal, with ΔLLR = −50.3, ΔBIC = −109.1 and ΔAIC = −104.5, where negative values favour the model (p. e2). The skew and kurtosis of log field size matched the model but not the log-normal, for which both should be near zero (p. 1111). Read from Fig. 1E (p. 1112), the data have skew ≈ −0.72 and kurtosis ≈ 1.65. Model simulations centre near skew ≈ −0.75 and kurtosis ≈ 1.3, with kurtosis spread ≈ 0.5 to 2.3, and the log-normal simulations sit near 0.
- 6 m tunnel. Field sizes do not differ from the Rayleigh prediction (Kolmogorov-Smirnov p = 0.6, statistic 0.1, 40 cells; Fig. 5D legend, p. 1117).
- 48 m rat maze. "Quantitative agreement", with matching skew and kurtosis (p. 1111; Figure S1, supplement, not read).
- 40 m mouse track. "Qualitative agreement with the Rayleigh distribution" (p. 1111).
- 2D and 3D. The text describes 1D slices as "well fitted by the Rayleigh distribution", 2D field areas as "precisely" exponential, and counts as "well fitted by a Poisson distribution" (p. 1114). The 1D slices, 2D slices and volumes in 3D were "verified by the data" (p. 1114). The main text gives no test statistic for these. The comparisons are model histograms with error bars from simulation.
- Control model. A model that matches the empirical 3D field volumes and peak rates but has no underlying Gaussian statistics fails to match the 1D and 2D slice sizes (p. 1114; Figures S2A-B) and the 3D EC curve (p. 1116; Figure S2C).

Shape predictions used the σ and θ fitted to field arrangement only, with no refit (p. 1114).

- Peaks per field, 200 m tunnel. Data and model agree (Fig. 3B, p. 1115), with ≈95% of fields single-peaked, ≈5% double-peaked and ≈1% or fewer with three peaks.
- Size versus peak rate. Peak rate rises with field size as an approximate power law (p. 1114). Exponents printed in Fig. 3 (p. 1115) are 1.42 in the data against 1.46 in the model for the 200 m tunnel, and 1.75 against 1.71 for the 3D room. The model exponent printed for the 6 m tunnel is 1.72 (Fig. 4C, p. 1116).
- Boundary slopes follow a Rayleigh distribution, as predicted (p. 1114; Fig. 3E).
- 3D boundary curvatures match the model (p. 1114; Figures S2D-E, supplement, not read).
- Euler characteristic. In 1D it was in "excellent agreement" with Eq. 8 using the σ fitted earlier (p. 1114; Fig. 3F). In 3D it was non-monotonic as predicted and "precisely followed" Eq. 10 (p. 1116; Fig. 3G). In 2D it showed "excellent agreement" with Eq. 9 on 20 rat cells (p. 1116; Fig. 3H). See the reading cautions below about the 2D panel.

#### How σ and θ change with environment size

- The correlation length σ "increases sublinearly with the size of the environment", and the normalized threshold θ "decreases with the size of the environment" (p. 1116).
- Fig. 4B (p. 1116) plots log σ against log linear environment size, with straight-line fits. The printed slopes are γ = 0.24 for the bat tunnels (6 to 200 m) and γ = 0.53 for the rat arenas (0.5 to 18.6 m²). The plotted points sit at the natural log of tunnel length and, for the arenas, of the square root of floor area (≈, my check of the point positions). Equivalently, σ grows roughly as length^0.24 in the tunnels and as area^0.27 in the arenas [derived].
- θ falls from 2.4 (6 m) to 1.2 (130 m) and 1.1 (200 m) in the tunnels. In the arenas it falls from 2.2 (0.5 m²) to 1.6 (2.2 m²) and 1.2 (8.2 and 18.6 m²) (printed in Figs. 4A-B).
- Together these trends raise the average number of fields per cell with environment size. The authors say this fits single fields in small environments and a sublinear growth of field size (p. 1116, citing Figure S4).
- The sublinear growth of σ and the fall of θ push average propensity in opposite directions, so propensity "varies only weakly with environment size" (p. 1116; Figure S3, supplement, not read). The Discussion instead summarises that "field sizes increase with environment size, propensity decreases, and field shapes become more heterogeneous" (p. 1117).
- The authors do not explain the trends. They propose testing whether efficient-coding or optimal-storage theory could account for them (p. 1118).

#### Field shape and threshold

- A higher θ gives fewer multi-peaked fields and pushes the size-versus-peak-rate exponent toward 2, the value for parabolic fields. The model therefore predicts more stereotyped field shapes in smaller environments (p. 1116; Fig. 5, p. 1117).
- Fig. 5B gives the multi-peak field frequency as ≈5.8% at the 200 m tunnel's θ = 1.1 and ≈0.4% at the 6 m tunnel's θ = 2.4. Fig. 5C gives an exponent of ≈1.47 at the 200 m point and ≈1.74 at the 6 m point, rising to ≈1.78 at θ = 2.9.
- In the 6 m tunnel, as predicted, fields were more stereotyped than in the 200 m tunnel, multi-peaked fields were "practically absent", and the statistics agreed quantitatively with the model (p. 1116; Figs. 4C, 5D).
- The authors' conclusion is that "place fields in small environments differ from those observed in a similarly sized portion of a larger environment" (p. 1117).

#### What the model implies for very large fields

What the paper says:

- Field size grows with environment size, but sublinearly (pp. 1116-1117).
- The authors replace the earlier heuristic log-normal fit of the bat field-size distribution with the thresholded-process account. The log-normal "lacked a principled rationale" (p. 1111).
- Across dimensions, "the distribution becomes more concentrated around zero and develops a longer tail" as D increases (Fig. 2J legend, p. 1114).
- The 3D analysis removed fields larger than the mean plus 3 SD as probable merged fields (p. e3). The largest 3D fields are therefore excluded by construction.

Field-size ranges in the figures (all ≈):

- 200 m tunnel, ≈0.5 to 32 m, with the mode near 4 to 5 m (Fig. 1C).
- 6 m tunnel, ≈0.2 to 3.3 m, with the mode near 1 m (Fig. 5D).
- 18.6 m² arena, 1D slices up to ≈2.3 m and field areas up to ≈3 m² (Figs. 2C-D).
- 40 m mouse track, up to ≈1 to 1.3 m, with the mode near 0.25 to 0.3 m (Fig. 1F; the axis is in cm).

Tails of Eq. 1 [derived; high-threshold limit, not stated in the paper]:

- 1D. P(s > k × mean) = exp(−πk²/4). That is ≈0.04 for k = 2, ≈8 × 10⁻⁴ for k = 3 and ≈3 × 10⁻⁹ for k = 5.
- 2D. P(area > k × mean) = e^(−k).
- 3D. P(volume > k × mean) = exp(−(Γ(5/2)·k)^(2/3)). That is ≈0.08 for k = 3 and ≈0.004 for k = 10.

Under the model, a 1D field many times the mean size is effectively absent. In 3D the tail is much heavier.

#### How many fields a cell has in large spaces

- The mean number of fields per cell rises with environment size (p. 1116).
- In 1D the expected count per cell is Eq. 8, (L/σ)/(2π)·e^(−θ²/2) + (1 − Φ(θ)) (pp. 1114, e3).
- At high threshold, counts per cell are Poisson (p. 1114). In the 18.6 m² arena the count histogram, conditioned on at least one field, has its mode at about 3 fields (≈) and runs to about 9 (≈) (Fig. 2E, p. 1113).
- Per unit size, propensity varies only weakly with environment size (p. 1116) or decreases (p. 1117). See above.
- Cell-to-cell variation. In the bat tunnel, a cell's field number and field sizes were correlated between the two flight directions even though the fields remapped. The authors attribute this to variation in θ across cells. An extended model with a distribution of thresholds reproduced the correlations while keeping the size and gap distributions of the basic model (p. 1116; Figure S4, supplement, not read). They suggest the threshold may vary through intrinsic cell properties or inhibitory input. They call this "consistent with the recent observation that propensity varies across cells also in rodents", citing Lee et al. 2020 (p. 1116).

#### Rich et al. 2014 and the gamma-Poisson account

The paper does not compare its model with the gamma-Poisson account. The words "gamma", "negative binomial" and "overdispersion" do not occur in the article. Rich et al. 2014 appears in three roles. It is one of the experiments showing multiple irregular fields in large environments (pp. 1110, 1116). It supplies the 48 m maze dataset, fitted on mean size and count (p. e2). Its field-size distribution matches the model, including skew and kurtosis (p. 1111; Figure S1, supplement, not read).

With one θ for all cells, the model's counts are Poisson (p. 1114). The threshold-distribution extension (p. 1116) is the paper's only route to cell-specific field rates. The main text does not test that extension against count overdispersion or relate it to a gamma distribution [inference].

### What it concludes about the inputs to CA1

Statements:

- Summary: the results "further suggest that synaptic projections to CA1 are predominantly random" (p. 1110).
- Discussion: CA1 place fields, "in small and large environments alike, are compatible with predominantly random synaptic connections into CA1". The reason given is that random projections from a large enough number of spatially selective cells "necessarily produce an input to each CA1 cell that varies spatially as a realization of a random Gaussian process", by the multivariate central limit theorem (p. 1118; Methods S2, supplement, not read). The input tuning can range "from regular tuning curves that tile the environment to heterogeneous and irregular tuning curves with a distance-dependent covariance function". The Discussion ends that "the broad success of the model across datasets and statistics suggests that randomness, rather than specific design, governs the synaptic organization of inputs to CA1" (p. 1118).
- The authors set this against the earlier view, motivated by stereotyped fields in small environments, that place selectivity comes from highly organized connectivity (refs 44-46). They say the irregular CA1 firing in large environments is "inconsistent with this view" (p. 1118).
- The randomness could reflect a fixed anatomy shaped by development (ref 47). It could also reflect the cumulative effect of many randomly occurring, non-Hebbian plasticity events at CA3-to-CA1 synapses that entorhinal input triggers (refs 48-50, behavioural-timescale plasticity) (p. 1118).
- The authors speculate that under richer behavioural conditions, activity-dependent plasticity adds more intricate features "on top of a scaffold of random synaptic weights" (p. 1118).

The evidence is indirect. It consists of the model's fit to output field statistics across the bat and rodent datasets in 1D, 2D and 3D, plus the central-limit argument that random weights produce Gaussian input. The paper reports no anatomical or connectivity measurement. The only alternative model it tests is the non-Gaussian control that matches field volumes and peak rates (Figure S2). By the universality result on p. 1111, field statistics depend on the input covariance only through σ [inference: so these fits cannot identify the input tuning or the shape of the covariance].

On coding, the authors do not cast random and optimized codes as alternatives. Random Gaussian-process codes "can generate a highly efficient coding scheme, with capacity that increases exponentially with the number of neurons" (ref 29). They write that the structure of CA1 tuning "might be shaped to optimize coding efficiency", or might serve associative memory (ref 27) (p. 1118).

### Population similarity, other regions, and the bat multiscale code

Population-level similarity and change with distance. The paper has no population-vector, decoding or similarity-versus-distance analysis. The only distance-dependent quantity in it is the single-cell input covariance r(x − x′). The authors assume it is translation invariant (pp. 1111, e1) and simulate it as Gaussian (p. e2). The field statistics are insensitive to its shape beyond σ (p. 1111). The paper does not say how similarity between population vectors should fall with distance, and it does not measure it. The nearest statements are that the multiple fields of single cells "as well as those of the whole population" vary in size and shape (p. 1110). It also says small-environment fields differ from fields in an equally sized part of a large environment (p. 1117).

CA3, subiculum and other regions. All analysed data are dorsal CA1 (p. 1116).

- CA3 appears in three roles: as an input source to CA1 together with entorhinal cortex (p. 1111), as the site of earlier attractor models with multiple random fields (refs 26-27, p. 1110), and as the presynaptic side of plastic CA3-to-CA1 synapses (p. 1118).
- There are no CA3, entorhinal or subicular data. The word "subiculum" does not occur. The irregular 3D entorhinal and grid-cell results (refs 13-14) are cited only as examples of irregular tuning (p. 1110).
- Using one σ per dataset is justified by the dorsal CA1 localization. The authors expect "some variation in these parameters across cells and anatomical regions", citing Kjelstrup et al. 2008 among others (p. 1116).

Bat multiscale coding (Eliav et al. 2021):

- Eliav's 200, 130 and 6 m tunnel data are the core 1D datasets (pp. 1111, 1116, e2).
- The introduction credits Eliav et al. with showing that multi-peaked, multi-scale tuning helps coding efficiency (refs 21 and 29, p. 1110).
- The paper reinterprets the skewed bat field-size distribution, which had been "fitted heuristically to a log-normal distribution", as the Rayleigh-like distribution of threshold-crossing lengths. The log-normal fits worse by likelihood, BIC, AIC and the skew and kurtosis of log sizes (pp. 1111, e2). The paper does not say who made the log-normal fit. The dataset is Eliav's.
- In the model, multi-scale fields within one cell come from random excursions of one process, with no designed mixture of scales [inference]. The main text does not analyse within-cell field-size ratios separately.
- The correlation of field number and size between flight directions, despite remapping, is a finding of Eliav et al. The paper explains it by cell-specific thresholds (p. 1116).
- The paper does not dispute the efficiency argument. It suggests random Gaussian-process codes can themselves be efficient (p. 1118).

### Predictions stated by the authors

- 1D slices through 2D or 3D fields follow the 1D (Rayleigh) size law, and 2D slices through 3D fields follow the 2D (exponential) law. Both were tested (p. 1114).
- Fields can have several local maxima, with a predicted distribution of peak counts. Tested (p. 1114).
- Peak rate and field size follow a power law with a predicted exponent, which approaches 2 at high threshold. Tested (pp. 1114, 1116).
- Boundary slopes follow a Rayleigh distribution, and 3D boundary curvatures follow the model. Tested (p. 1114).
- The EC as a function of threshold follows Eqs. 8-10. Tested (pp. 1114, 1116).
- Lowering the threshold, for example by optogenetic depolarization during behaviour, should unmask new fields. Valero et al. 2022 is "in qualitative agreement" (pp. 1114-1115).
- Field shapes are more stereotyped in smaller environments because θ is higher there. Tested in the 6 m tunnel (pp. 1116-1117).
- Fields in a small environment differ from those in an equally sized part of a larger one (p. 1117).
- Open, not tested: whether the parameter trends follow from a theory of efficient coding or optimal storage capacity (p. 1118).

### Limitations stated by the authors

- The universal formulas (Eq. 1, exponential gaps, Poisson counts) hold in the high-threshold limit. Exact predictions need simulation (pp. 1111, 1112).
- The model uses one σ and θ per dataset. Variation across cells and anatomical regions "is to be expected". Threshold variation is handled only in the extended model (p. 1116).
- All data come from highly familiar, uniform environments, so the stationarity of the process may be specific to those conditions (p. 1118).
- The mechanistic origin of the random input is open (p. 1118).
- The 40 m track offered only a field-size histogram, and the fit there is qualitative (pp. 1111, e2). The 2D slice and EC analyses use 20 cells. Other 2D results rest on statistics published by Harland et al. (p. e2).
- Data-processing choices include rectification at 0.5 Hz and 1 Hz, smoothing for peak counts, and removal of large 3D outliers (pp. e2-e3). The paper examines the effect of rectification in Figure S5A.

### Reading cautions (my own observations)

1. Animal on the 40 m virtual track. The main text and Fig. 1F say mice (pp. 1111-1112). The STAR Methods say rats (p. e2). The source, Lee et al. 2020, is a mouse study.
2. Propensity. The Results say it "varies only weakly" with environment size (p. 1116). The Discussion says it "decreases" (p. 1117).

Read at face value, 1D slices through 3D fields reach ≈4.5 m (Fig. 2H), 2D slice areas reach ≈30 m² (Fig. 2I) and field volumes stay below ≈0.07 m³ (Fig. 2J). 4. Field counts from the Fig. 4A values. Eq. 8 with L = 200 m, σ = 2.4 and θ = 1.1 gives ≈7.4 expected fields per cell [derived]. One account gives each CA1 cell an independent realization of a smooth random Gaussian process over space and makes the cell fire, in proportion to the excess, wherever its realization exceeds a threshold.
